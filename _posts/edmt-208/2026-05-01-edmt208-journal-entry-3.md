### Key question I chose
**In what ways can you integrate AI into your teaching of mathematics?**

After reading the Unit 4 materials, I became more aware that technology is not just something placed beside mathematics for convenience. It has always shaped how people count, calculate, represent, verify, and communicate mathematical ideas. Hansson (2020) explains that the relationship between mathematics and technology is reciprocal. Technology depends on mathematics, but mathematics also depends on tools, from paper and blackboards to computers used for calculations, validation, and even searching for counterexamples.

This made me think about artificial intelligence more carefully. AI should not be treated as a shortcut that replaces mathematical thinking. It should be used as a tool that can help students see, test, explain, and revise their thinking. In my own teaching, especially in Statistics and Probability, I want AI to support the parts of mathematics learning where students often need more feedback, examples, and confidence.

## How I understand AI as a teaching support

One important learning for me is that AI in mathematics education is not limited to one kind of tool. It can appear as an intelligent tutor, an adaptive learning system, a chatbot, an automated feedback tool, a data analysis assistant, or even a platform that helps teachers understand students’ learning patterns. Recent reviews describe AI tools in mathematics education as useful for personalized learning, real-time feedback, adaptive learning paths, assessment support, and student engagement (Nguyen & Pham, 2025; Yıldız & Körpeoğlu, 2025).

As a teacher, I find this promising because one of the hardest parts of classroom teaching is responding to the different speeds of learners. Some students understand a procedure quickly but cannot explain why it works. Others can explain the idea but get lost in the computation. Some students do not ask questions because they are shy or afraid of being judged. AI can help by giving students another space to ask, practice, and receive feedback before or after class.

But I also think the teacher must remain central. AI can generate explanations, examples, and practice questions, but it does not know the full story of my students. It does not see who is tired, who is silent but capable, who is copying without understanding, or who needs encouragement before correction. That is still the work of the teacher.

## Where I can integrate AI in my mathematics class

I can integrate AI first in lesson preparation. Before teaching a topic, I can use AI to generate several versions of examples, from basic to challenging. For instance, if I am teaching measures of central tendency, I can ask for data sets where the mean and median tell different stories. This can help me prepare examples that show why interpretation matters. In Statistics, this is very useful because students often think that once they compute the answer, the work is already done. AI can help me design situations where students must explain what the number means.

Second, I can use AI to support formative assessment. After a lesson on hypothesis testing, I can ask AI to help draft short exit questions, common misconceptions, and sample student responses. Then I can revise these materials based on my actual class context. For example, if students keep confusing the null hypothesis with the research claim, I can prepare targeted practice items that focus only on that misunderstanding. In this way, AI becomes a planning assistant, not the final author of my lesson.

Third, AI can help students practice mathematical communication. In my classes, I do not only want students to compute. I want them to explain. For example, after solving a probability problem, students may ask AI to check whether their explanation is clear, then they can compare the AI feedback with the teacher’s comments. The goal is not for AI to give them the answer. The goal is for students to learn how to write mathematical reasoning in complete and understandable sentences.

Fourth, I can use AI for differentiated practice. If one group of students is still struggling with interpreting graphs, while another group is ready for more complex data-based questions, AI can help me prepare different sets of practice tasks. This is especially helpful in a classroom where students do not all need the same kind of support at the same time. The reading on AI integration reminded me that adaptive and personalized learning is one of the major directions in mathematics education research (Nguyen & Pham, 2025). In practice, that means I can give more appropriate support without making every learner follow only one pace.

Fifth, I can connect AI with my own use of technology as a researcher. When I use R, Python, spreadsheets, or visualization tools, I already rely on technology to make mathematical and statistical work clearer. AI can help students see that technology is not only for getting quick answers. It can also help organize data, check patterns, simulate outcomes, and communicate results. For example, in a lesson on sampling, students can use a spreadsheet or simple simulation, then use AI prompts to help them reflect on why samples vary and why larger samples usually give more stable estimates.

## What I need to be careful about

At the same time, I do not want to be careless. The readings also made me think about the challenges of AI in mathematics teaching. AI can produce wrong solutions, overly confident explanations, or steps that look convincing but are mathematically weak. Students may also become dependent on it if they use it only to finish homework quickly. This is dangerous because mathematics learning requires struggle, checking, and revision.

There are also issues of access and fairness. Not all students have stable internet, good devices, or the same level of digital confidence. If I require AI use without considering these realities, then technology may widen the gap instead of helping close it. Privacy is also important. I should not ask students to upload personal information, grades, or sensitive class data into AI tools. If AI is used in class, the task must be designed with clear limits.

Because of these concerns, I think AI use in mathematics should follow three simple rules in my classroom. First, students must show their own reasoning. Second, AI output must be checked, not blindly accepted. Third, the teacher must decide when AI is useful and when it distracts from the lesson.

## What I will try in my own teaching

One activity I want to try is an “AI as a checking partner” routine. Students will first solve a problem on their own. Then, they will use AI only after writing their initial answer. They will ask AI to identify possible errors or unclear parts of their explanation. After that, they will revise their work and write a short note answering: What did I change, and why?

I like this routine because it keeps the student’s thinking first. AI enters after effort has already been made. It also teaches students that checking is part of mathematics. In a way, this connects with the broader idea from Unit 4 that technology can extend what we do in mathematics, but it should not remove responsibility from the learner.

In the end, I do not see AI as the future replacement of the mathematics teacher. I see it as one more tool that must be handled with judgment. A calculator can compute, but it cannot decide what matters in a problem. A graphing tool can draw, but it cannot guarantee understanding. AI can explain, generate, and respond, but it cannot replace the teacher’s care, discernment, and knowledge of students.

<p style="text-align:center;"><em>AI can support mathematics learning, but the heart of the lesson must still be human reasoning.</em></p>

That is the balance I want to bring into my classroom.
