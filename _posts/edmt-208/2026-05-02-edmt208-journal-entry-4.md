### Key question I chose
**What should be the mathematics that Filipino students need to learn? Justify your choice.**

After going through the Unit 5 materials, I found myself returning to one simple answer: Filipino students need to learn mathematics that helps them make sense of life and make responsible decisions. I do not mean that the usual topics should be removed. Students still need number sense, algebra, geometry, measurement, data, probability, and proof. But these should not be taught as isolated school requirements. They should be taught as tools for understanding situations, questioning information, solving problems, and participating more intelligently in society.

This is important to me because many students still experience mathematics as a subject of procedures. They learn how to compute, substitute, simplify, and solve. Those skills matter, but they are not enough. A student may know how to calculate a mean but still be confused by a misleading graph. A student may know how to solve for *x* but still struggle to compare loan offers, interpret a survey result, or understand risk during a typhoon. For me, that gap is where mathematics education must become more honest.

## Mathematics as decision-making

One idea that stayed with me from the MATATAG Mathematics curriculum is that numeracy is not only about computation. It is also the ability to access, use, interpret, and communicate mathematical information in different adult-life situations. The curriculum also emphasizes that Filipino learners should become mathematically proficient and critical problem solvers (DepEd, 2023). I think this is the kind of mathematics Filipino students need most: mathematics that leads to judgment, not just answers.

In a Philippine classroom, this can begin with very ordinary situations. Students can examine a family budget and ask how much is left after food, fare, load, school contributions, and emergency expenses. They can compare prices in a sari-sari store or market and decide which option gives better value. They can read a barangay health report and ask whether a rise in dengue cases is meaningful or just part of regular fluctuation. They can look at rainfall data and discuss why some areas are more vulnerable to flooding. These are not small examples. They are the actual situations where mathematics becomes part of daily decision-making.

This is also why I think data and probability should be given stronger attention. Students today are surrounded by numbers: rankings, percentages, survey results, social media claims, health statistics, weather forecasts, grades, prices, and algorithm-generated recommendations. If they cannot interpret these numbers carefully, they can easily be misled. A mathematics classroom should train them to ask basic but powerful questions:

1. Where did the data come from?
2. Who was included and who was left out?
3. What does the graph show, and what does it hide?
4. Is the conclusion supported by the evidence?
5. What decision should be made from this information?

These questions are mathematical, but they are also civic. They help students become more careful citizens.

## Mathematics that connects to real Philippine problems

UNESCO's *Mathematics for Action* reminded me that mathematics has a role in science-based decision-making, especially in problems connected to sustainable development (UNESCO, 2022). In the Philippines, this is not abstract. We experience disasters, public health risks, educational inequality, food insecurity, transportation problems, and uneven access to technology. Many of these issues require measurement, modelling, estimation, optimization, and data interpretation.

For example, when discussing SDG 13 on climate action, students can work with local rainfall patterns, temperature records, or flood-risk maps. They do not need to begin with advanced models. Even simple line graphs, averages, percentages, and comparisons can help them see patterns. When discussing SDG 3 on good health and well-being, students can examine anonymized school clinic data, dengue case trends, or vaccination coverage. When discussing SDG 4 on quality education, students can analyze attendance, test scores, access to gadgets, or internet connectivity in their own school context.

What matters is that mathematics is not presented as detached from social life. It should help students see that every table, graph, and formula has consequences. A wrong interpretation can lead to a poor decision. A careful interpretation can support better planning.

## Foundations still matter

At the same time, I do not think applied mathematics should replace foundational mathematics. Filipino students still need fluency. They still need to know how to operate with numbers, manipulate expressions, understand shapes, reason with functions, and communicate solutions clearly. The SEI-DOST and MATHTED framework describes mathematical empowerment as developing critical and analytical thinking, supported by problem solving, mathematical communication, reasoning, and mathematical connections (SEI-DOST & MATHTED, 2011). That balance is important.

A student cannot interpret a loan computation well without understanding percentages. A student cannot read a graph properly without understanding scale. A student cannot model population growth without understanding functions. A student cannot evaluate a claim about probability without understanding chance. So the answer is not less mathematics. The answer is mathematics taught with clearer purpose.

I also think this is where many students lose confidence. They are asked to perform procedures before they understand why those procedures matter. If the lesson begins from a real situation, then the need for the procedure becomes more visible. For example, the formula for the mean is easier to appreciate when students are comparing daily expenses, rainfall measurements, or quiz scores. Linear functions make more sense when they describe jeepney fares, water consumption, or savings over time. Probability becomes more meaningful when it is connected to weather forecasts, games of chance, health risk, or sampling.

## Mathematics and technology

The mathematics Filipino students need should also include wise use of technology. But I think this must be understood carefully. Technology integration should not mean that every classroom must depend on expensive devices. In many far-flung areas, the most available technologies may be a teacher's phone, a basic calculator, printed tables, manila paper, improvised measuring tools, or offline files shared through Bluetooth or USB. These can still support good mathematics learning when the task is well designed.

For me, the goal is not to make students dependent on technology. The goal is to help them use tools intelligently. A spreadsheet can help students organize data. A calculator can help them focus on interpretation when the computation is repetitive. A graphing tool can help them see the shape of a function. But students should still know what the tool is doing and whether the output makes sense. Technology should extend thinking, not replace it.

This is especially important now because students are entering a world where artificial intelligence, dashboards, automated systems, and online information are becoming common. If they do not understand data, scale, uncertainty, and bias, they may trust outputs too quickly. Mathematics education should help them pause and ask: Is this reasonable? What assumptions are being made? What evidence supports this result?

## What I want my students to carry with them

As a teacher, I want my students to leave mathematics class with more than procedures. I want them to carry a habit of careful thinking. I want them to see mathematics when they budget money, read news, interpret school data, compare choices, evaluate claims, and solve community problems. I want them to understand that mathematics is not only for those who will become engineers, scientists, or mathematicians. It is also for future parents, voters, workers, entrepreneurs, teachers, health professionals, farmers, and public servants.

So, what mathematics should Filipino students learn? They should learn mathematics that is foundational, contextual, critical, and humane. Foundational, because basic skills matter. Contextual, because students learn better when mathematics speaks to their world. Critical, because society is full of numbers that need to be questioned. Humane, because mathematics education should help learners participate in life with confidence, not make them feel excluded from it.

In the end, the mathematics Filipino students need is mathematics that helps them think clearly about real problems.

<p style="text-align:center;"><em>Mathematics should not only teach students how to get an answer; it should also teach them how to judge whether an answer is useful, truthful, and responsible.</em></p>

That is the kind of mathematics I want to teach more deliberately.
